Quiet

Susan Cain

Read: November 27, 2025 • Rating: 7/10

Read my thoughts

How do introverts function in a world built for extroverts? Is it really that overwhelming, or are we overcomplicating it? Being introverted means having a more sensitive nervous system — noise, bright light, crowded rooms with too many conversations all cost more energy than they seem to. Our fuel comes from quiet: doing things alone, studying, walking, thinking. The world mostly asks everyone to perform as an extrovert, forgetting that the ability to focus deeply, observe closely, and stay calm under noise is its own kind of advantage. I'm not undervaluing extroverts here — just giving introverts their due.

The book grounds all of this in real research, more than I expected was sitting behind personality types. Cain also writes about how introverts love: because their circle is already small, they tend to give everything to the people inside it, sometimes more than needed, sometimes to their own detriment — getting hurt more easily when a partner spends time socializing instead of sharing "us-time."

She doesn't skip introverted children either — how hard it can be to meet their needs, and how often they're wiser than their age from early on. This isn't just a book about introverts. It's closer to a decoder for a world only introverts really understand from the inside.

Inferno

Dan Brown

Read: April 2, 2026 • Rating: 8/10

Read my thoughts

Have you ever thought about overpopulation? Is it a global problem? Could it be a reason humans go extinct one day? I hadn't really considered it before this book. Even with birth rates declining, people are living far longer thanks to better quality of life — and that math still adds up to a real strain.

The book leans heavily on the idea of denial — seeing something horrible happening and looking away because it's easier, because it's not your problem. In Uzbek there's a word, "odamgarchilik," that captures the opposite of that: helping each other, using empathy to resist that instinct to look away. We're not built to live entirely for ourselves. We're built for companionship, and acts of service humble our ego in a way nothing else does.

I like how Dan Brown weaves history into the present — myths, cities, symbols hidden in plain sight in places we think we already know. The whole book unfolds in just 24 hours, and he holds the tension the entire way, revealing the full picture only at the very end.

"The darkest places in hell are reserved for those who maintain neutrality in times of moral crisis." That line stuck with me. Denial, at scale, becomes its own kind of pandemic.

Before the Coffee Gets Cold

Toshikazu Kawaguchi

Read: April 28, 2026 • Rating: 6/10

Read my thoughts

Honestly, the title is the only reason I picked this up. Do you have regrets? Maybe someone you've lost that you want one more meeting with. Maybe one thing in the past you wish you could undo. We're simple yet complicated creatures — we regret, we lose, we want people back, we want to do better next time. Why is it so hard to accept what's already happened to us?

The harsh truth: our past is unchangeable, our future unknown, and all we really have is the present — yet we still try to control what we can't. The book's core idea is that going back doesn't change what happened, but it can change how you see it, and how you think about your own decisions going forward. A little cliché at times, but it lands the point clearly: don't do what you'll regret, and don't keep regretting what's already done.

As for the coffee in the title — you'll have to read it before yours gets cold.

A Little Life

Hanya Yanagihara

Read: July 11, 2026 • Rating: 7/10

Read my thoughts

Assault. Abuse. Trauma. Childhood. Coping mechanisms. I wish these words would not come together ever again. Unfortunately, this is life. Reading this book convinced me further that the body keeps the score — and not just for the main character, but for every character in it. They've all carried early trauma that shaped them in ways that are hard to undo, chemically and emotionally.

Alongside trauma, people develop coping mechanisms — some healthier than others. I run when I'm in distress. Others sleep, eat, or turn to things that hurt them physically in the short term. We're complicated that way.

The book is beautiful and heavy. I read it at a low point, and it became a kind of companion in sorrow — a "things happen, be gentle with yourself" kind of book. Not for everyone, especially if you're easily triggered by trauma on the page, but worth trying. We all have a little life — sometimes easier, sometimes harder to live. But we always live. And we want others to keep living too.
