Inferno

By: Dan Brown — Read: April 2, 2026 • Rating: 8/10

Have you ever thought about overpopulation? Is it a global problem? Could it be a reason humans go extinct one day? I hadn't really considered it before this book. Even with birth rates declining, people are living far longer thanks to better quality of life — and that math still adds up to a real strain.

The book leans heavily on the idea of denial — seeing something horrible happening and looking away because it's easier, because it's not your problem. In Uzbek there's a word, "odamgarchilik," that captures the opposite of that: helping each other, using empathy to resist that instinct to look away. We're not built to live entirely for ourselves. We're built for companionship, and acts of service humble our ego in a way nothing else does.

I like how Dan Brown weaves history into the present — myths, cities, symbols hidden in plain sight in places we think we already know. The whole book unfolds in just 24 hours, and he holds the tension the entire way, revealing the full picture only at the very end.

"The darkest places in hell are reserved for those who maintain neutrality in times of moral crisis." That line stuck with me. Denial, at scale, becomes its own kind of pandemic.
